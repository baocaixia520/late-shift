Cam Studio Roll-Ups Are Quiet, and That Is the Point

Fourteen acquisitions since January, almost none announced. The buyers are small funds paying three to five times earnings, and the sellers are operators who never wanted a press release.

By Marla Vance / 14 November 2026 / 2 min read

Fourteen cam studio acquisitions have closed since January, according to three brokers and a fund that tracks the segment. Eleven were never announced. Two buyers have rolled up five studios each into single billing entities, and a third is buying the traffic rather than the roster, which is a different transaction with a different multiple.

The numbers are small and consistent. Targets run $1.2 million to $6 million in annual revenue, 8 to 40 contracted performers, and a two-year history of platform payouts that the buyer can verify line by line. Multiples land between three and five times trailing twelve-month earnings, with a heavier earn-out than a typical media deal because the buyer is underwriting the roster, not the brand.

What the roll-up actually buys

Three things: a second billing relationship with the same platform, a compliance function that can be shared across studios, and traffic that can be cross-promoted without paying an affiliate. The first two are boring and defensible. The third is where the pitch gets optimistic, because cross-promotion works until the audience notices that six studios are one studio.

“We are not buying content. We are buying the plumbing that lets twenty performers be paid on one invoice.”Partner at a fund with two studio holdings

Sellers have their own reasons. Platform payout terms have tightened twice in eighteen months, chargeback reserves now run 5 to 10 percent of volume, and a solo owner with 12 performers is spending more time on statements than on production. Selling at 3.5x is a way of converting an operating headache into cash without hiring a compliance manager.

The risk is concentration. A roll-up with five studios on the same platform, the same processor and the same billing descriptor is not diversified; it is one account with five product lines. Two of the buyers have already been asked to split their descriptors after a shared outage took every studio offline for a weekend.

  • Platform dependency: how much of the revenue comes from one site
  • Chargeback history by studio, not by group
  • Performer contracts: term, exclusivity and what transfers
  • Compliance records, and who owns them after closing

Key takeaways

  • Fourteen studio acquisitions have closed this year; eleven were never announced.
  • Targets run $1.2M–$6M in revenue and sell for 3–5x earnings, with heavy earn-outs.
  • The real asset is shared billing and compliance, not the content library.
