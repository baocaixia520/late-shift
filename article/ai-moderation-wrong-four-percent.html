AI Moderation Is Fast, Cheap and Wrong Four Percent of the Time

Automated review clears 96 percent of cam-stream frames correctly. The remaining 4 percent is a queue of humans, a set of appeals and a legal exposure nobody has priced.

By Tobias Lund / 15 October 2026 / 2 min read

Three cam platforms now run automated frame review on live streams, with a human queue behind it. The vendors quote 96 to 98 percent accuracy on a labelled test set. On live traffic, the number that matters is the false positive rate, because a false positive is a stream taken down while it is being paid for.

A 4 percent false positive rate on 6,000 hours of live streaming a week is 240 hours of wrongly interrupted sessions. At an average of $0.90 per minute in tips, that is roughly $13,000 a week in refunds and goodwill, before the model, the queue and the appeals process are paid for.

The three costs behind the model

Human review of flagged frames, which is the largest line and does not shrink as accuracy improves because the volume of streams grows. Appeal handling, which needs a person with authority to reverse a decision. And the tail of sessions that a model cannot classify at all — new performers, unusual lighting, costumes — which defaults to manual review.

“The model is right most of the time. The problem is that the wrong four percent are somebody's shift.”Trust and safety lead at a cam platform

The platforms that improved their numbers did it by narrowing the classifier rather than replacing it: detect a small set of unambiguous categories automatically, send everything ambiguous to a human, and never let an automated decision stand without a same-day appeal path.

The legal exposure is the part nobody prices. A takedown that cannot be appealed is a complaint waiting to happen, and complaints in this sector reach the processor before they reach the regulator, which is the more expensive route.

Key takeaways

  • Vendors claim 96–98 percent accuracy; the false positive rate is what costs money on live traffic.
  • 240 wrongly interrupted hours a week can mean about $13,000 in refunds before review costs.
  • Narrow automation plus a same-day appeal path beats a broader model with no reversal.
