A Three-Person Agency Rewrote Its Payout Ledger and Cut Disputes by Half

Weekly split statements, a written escrow rule and a single PDF replaced three spreadsheets and a group chat. Disputes fell 52 percent in six months.

By Marla Vance / 14 October 2026 / 2 min read

Kestrel Management is three people and twenty-two clients. Until March it tracked splits in two spreadsheets and a group chat, and paid out when the money cleared, which meant different clients were paid on different rhythms for reasons nobody could reconstruct a month later.

The rewrite took a weekend. Every booking now generates a single line in a ledger with four fields: gross, platform fee, agency commission and net to talent. Statements go out every Friday whether or not money moved, and an unpaid line stays visible until it clears.

The escrow rule, in one sentence

Client money sits in a separate account and is never used to cover agency costs; if a platform reverses a payment after a payout, the loss is carried by the agency, not deducted from the next statement. That single rule cost the agency about $3,400 in the first six months and removed the argument that used to arrive with every chargeback.

“Half our disputes were not about money. They were about not being able to see the money.”Founder, Kestrel Management

Disputes fell from roughly nine a month to four. The remaining four are mostly about commission on renewals, which is a contract question rather than a ledger question, and the agency now flags renewal dates ninety days out to stop them arriving as a surprise.

The cost is time: about six hours a week of bookkeeping that used to be two, and a bookkeeper at $600 a month. The founder's view is that the six hours bought back the twenty he used to spend answering messages about where the money was.

Key takeaways

  • One ledger line per booking — gross, platform fee, commission, net — replaced two spreadsheets and a group chat.
  • A written escrow rule that absorbs chargebacks cost $3,400 in six months and removed the largest dispute category.
  • Disputes fell 52 percent; the bookkeeping cost rose by roughly $600 a month and six hours a week.
