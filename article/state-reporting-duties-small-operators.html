State Reporting Duties Land on Small Operators

Two new filing requirements apply to businesses above a revenue threshold, and a four-person studio crosses it more easily than its owner expects.

By Priya Raghunathan / 2 November 2026 / 2 min read

Two states added annual reporting duties this year for businesses above a revenue threshold, and the definition of revenue includes platform payouts that arrive through a third party. For a studio that bills through a platform, that means gross bookings, not the net deposit.

The effect is to pull small operators into a filing regime designed for larger ones. A four-person studio with $2.4 million in gross bookings crosses a $2 million threshold that its owner assumed applied to net revenue of about $900,000.

What the filing asks for

Headcount, gross revenue, the jurisdictions in which content is distributed, the age-assurance method used, and a named responsible officer. None of it is onerous to produce if the books are clean. All of it is onerous to reconstruct after the deadline.

“We spent three weeks rebuilding a year of numbers that our processor had been holding all along.”Owner of a studio that filed late

The late-filing penalty is small; the consequence of a bad filing is not. A filing that understates revenue creates a record that a processor will treat as a discrepancy during the next review, and two studios said that review cost them a reserve increase.

The practical fix is boring: reconcile platform payouts to the merchant statement every month, keep a jurisdiction list alongside the content calendar, and nominate one person who owns the filing even if it takes four hours a year.

Key takeaways

  • Thresholds are based on gross bookings, including platform payouts, not net deposits.
  • A four-person studio at $2.4 million in bookings can be a large filer.
  • Monthly reconciliation to the merchant statement is the cheapest way to survive a filing.
