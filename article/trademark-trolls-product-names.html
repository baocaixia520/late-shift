Trademark Trolls Are Reaching for Product Names

A registration filed for a generic-sounding product name is cheap. Defending a listing is not. Three novelty brands have received demands this year over names they have used for a decade.

By Priya Raghunathan / 24 October 2026 / 2 min read

Three novelty brands have received demand letters this year over product names they have used since before the registrations existed. In each case the mark was filed recently, in a class that covers the product, by a party with no apparent connection to the industry.

The economics favour the sender. Filing a trademark costs a few hundred dollars. A demand letter costs a stamp. A response from a brand's lawyer costs $2,000 to $6,000, and a marketplace complaint that triggers a listing suspension costs far more in lost sales than the settlement being asked for.

What actually defends a name

Use, documented. A brand that can show ten years of invoices, packaging proofs and advertising carrying the name has a defensible position even against a later registration. The problem is that most brands have the usage and not the file, and reconstructing it under a deadline is expensive.

“We had nine years of invoices in a folder nobody had opened since 2019.”Brand manager who settled a demand

Two of the three brands settled for $4,000 to $12,000 rather than fight, because the marketplace suspension risk was larger than the demand. The third fought, spent $31,000, and kept the name after the registration was cancelled — a result that reads as a win and cost more than the settlement would have.

The preventive work is cheap: keep a digital file per product name with first-use date, packaging artwork, invoices and the listing URLs; register the names that matter in the classes that matter; and check the register before a launch rather than after.

Key takeaways

  • Recent filings on long-used product names are producing demand letters across the novelty sector.
  • A demand costs the sender a stamp and the brand $2,000–$6,000 to answer.
  • Documented first use is the defence; most brands have the usage but not the file.
